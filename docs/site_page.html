Open-data urban hydrology · Jaipur, Rajasthan

Jaipur Flood Vulnerability Index

Jaipur waterlogs in roughly the same places every monsoon. This model asks whether that is predictable from freely available data — and which of those places can least afford it.

Resolution 300 m grid Elevation SRTM ~30 m Rainfall ERA5, 2015–2025 Cost ₹0, no API keys

Status: built and tested, awaiting field validation. The pipeline runs end to end and its hydrology is verified against landscapes with known answers. It has not yet been tested against observed waterlogging in Jaipur, so no risk figures are published here. That step is in progress and is described below.

The question

Where does the water actually go?

Municipal drainage budgets are finite and have to be spent somewhere. Deciding where is usually done from complaint volume and institutional memory, which favours the neighbourhoods that complain loudest.

Water is less political. It runs downhill, collects in hollows, and pools where the ground is flat and a large area drains through. All three of those are computable from an elevation map using methods hydrologists have used since the 1970s. Layer on what is built there and how well served it is, and you get a ranked list of places to look at first.

Every input is free and needs no account, because a model nobody else can run is not much of a model.

Structure

Three components, openly weighted

Weights are a judgement call, so they live in one visible config file — and get stress-tested.

On the sensitivity component. Ward-level income data for Jaipur is not public at 300 m resolution, so this layer uses infrastructure proxies. Proxies can be wrong about real neighbourhoods — a new development has unpaved roads without deprivation; the walled city is dense without being poor. Outputs touching this component are described as areas with infrastructure characteristics associated with disadvantage, never as poor areas. The distinction matters because getting it wrong misdirects resources away from people who need them.
Method

The hydrology, in order

Each step depends on the one before it. Running them out of order produces a map that looks plausible and means nothing.

01

Fill the sinks

Priority-Flood (Barnes, Lehman & Mulla, 2014). Flood the landscape inward from its edges, always processing the lowest cell on the shoreline.

A single pixel wrongly 2 m too low becomes a pit with no downhill neighbour. Water flowing in vanishes, and everything downstream of it is wrong.
02

Route the flow

D8 steepest descent: each cell sends its water to whichever of its eight neighbours is steepest downhill.

Diagonal neighbours are 1.414× further away, so the comparison is on slope, not raw drop. Forgetting that biases every flow path diagonally — the classic version of this bug.
03

Accumulate

Process cells from highest to lowest, passing each running total downstream. One pass, no recursion.

A cell with accumulation 5 catches its own rain. A cell with 5,000 is a drainage channel collecting runoff from half a square kilometre. From the street they look identical.
04

Compute wetness

The Topographic Wetness Index (Beven & Kirkby, 1979), combining how much water arrives with how slowly it leaves.

High where the ground is both low and flat — which describes almost every chronic waterlogging spot in any city. Try it below.

Notice how much harder the slope slider pushes than the area slider. Flattening the ground drives the index up far faster than adding catchment does, because slope sits in the denominator. That is why a flat colony at the bottom of a gentle bowl floods more reliably than a steeper street with a much larger catchment above it.

Honesty

How you would know if it were wrong

A coloured map looks convincing whether or not it is correct. This is the part that decides.

Validation means collecting places in Jaipur that genuinely waterlogged — and places that genuinely stayed dry — from local news archives around known storm dates, from storm-day reports, and from walking the city with a notebook. Then checking whether the model scored the first group higher than the second.

The negatives are the hard part and the essential part. With flooded points only, a model that declares the whole city high risk scores perfectly. Nobody photographs a dry road, so they have to be sought deliberately.

The reported metric is AUC: given one flooded and one dry location picked at random, the probability the model ranked the flooded one higher. It is threshold-free and unaffected by class imbalance, which accuracy is not.

0.65

0.75

0.85

1.00
Suspect a bug or data leakage before celebrating.

Reported alongside it: a bootstrap 95% confidence interval, because a forty-point sample deserves error bars rather than a falsely precise number; and a comparison against single-variable baselines. If relative elevation alone matches the full composite index, the extra machinery is not earning its keep — and that gets reported rather than buried.

Robustness

Testing my own assumptions

The weights above are a judgement call. A fair critic points out that different weights give a different map, so why believe this one?

Rather than argue the point, the index is recomputed 200 times with every weight randomly perturbed by up to ±50% and renormalised. Cells that stay in the worst 10% under nearly every weighting are robust findings. Cells that jump around are artefacts of the assumptions, and reporting them as results would be overclaiming.

Only the robust cells reach the priority list. This is also where a real bug turned up: the first priority list ranked low-lying cells containing three buildings — hydrologically correct, operationally useless. Nobody needs an empty field de-watered. A minimum-exposure filter now applies to the action list, with the reasoning written into the code.

Limits

What this model cannot do

  • It is relative, not absoluteA score of 95 means “worse than 95% of Jaipur”. It never means a predicted depth. This is a prioritisation tool, not a hazard assessment.
  • 300 m cannot see a blocked drainStreet-level waterlogging often comes down to one clogged inlet. The model finds where terrain is against you; it cannot find the culvert.
  • The elevation data is from 2000SRTM predates two decades of construction and road-raising across Jaipur. Regraded ground is invisible to it.
  • No hydraulics, no drainage capacityThis is a static index, not a shallow-water simulation. A drain’s presence counts; its diameter and whether it is blocked are unknown and matter enormously.
  • OpenStreetMap coverage is uneven — and that cuts the wrong wayMapping effort tends to correlate with affluence, so under-mapped areas may appear to have less infrastructure and also less of everything else. This could bias the model against exactly the neighbourhoods it exists to find. It is the limitation I have not solved.

Running the model

# synthetic data, offline, about ten seconds
python run_pipeline.py --demo

# real downloaded data, about fifteen minutes
python run_pipeline.py

# verify the hydrology against landscapes with known answers
python -m pytest tests/ -v

The tests check that filling a pit raises it to the surrounding level, that water in a V-shaped valley collects on the valley floor, and that flow accumulation never invents water. If those fail, every map the project produces is wrong.
